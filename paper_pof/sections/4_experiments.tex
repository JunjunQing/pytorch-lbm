Three distinct regimes are identified (\Cref{tab:phase_diagram}):

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Symmetric regime} ($k < 1.2$): Low $\mathrm{We}$ or large $R^*$. Surface tension dominates, suppressing asymmetric spreading. Flat surface reference: $k = 1.0$ for all $\mathrm{We}$.
    
    \item \textbf{Moderately asymmetric regime} ($1.2 \leq k < 2.0$): Intermediate conditions. The momentum anisotropy driven by curved surface geometry creates preferential spreading in the azimuthal direction.
    
    \item \textbf{Highly asymmetric regime} ($k \geq 2.0$): High $\mathrm{We}$. Maximum asymmetry, with $k$ peaking near $\mathrm{We} \approx 15$ within the 2000-step window used here. Note that at $\mathrm{We}=15$ the asymmetry remains high ($k = 2.29$) even at $R^* = 3.0$, so this regime is not limited to small $R^*$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Weber Number Dependence}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{fig1_we_sweep.pdf}
    \caption{Asymmetry ratio $k_{max}$ vs Weber number at $R^*=1.0$ ($\alpha_{geo}=1.5$, $N=150$). Three regimes are identified: symmetric ($k<1.2$), asymmetric ($1.2\leq k<2.0$), and highly asymmetric ($k\geq 2.0$). The peak at $\mathrm{We}\approx 15$ corresponds to maximum asymmetry.}
    \label{fig:we_sweep}
\end{figure}

At fixed $R^* = 1.0$, the asymmetry ratio exhibits a non-monotonic dependence on $\mathrm{We}$ (\Cref{fig:we_sweep}). For low to moderate Weber numbers ($\mathrm{We} = 3$--$15$), $k$ increases monotonically from 1.62 to 3.30 as higher impact velocity amplifies the momentum redistribution driven by surface curvature. The asymmetry peaks near $\mathrm{We} \approx 15$ ($k = 3.30$), corresponding to the optimal balance between inertial spreading and curvature-induced asymmetry. Beyond this peak ($\mathrm{We} = 20$), $k$ decreases to 2.66. We note two caveats: (i) all values are windowed maxima over 2000 steps, and at $\mathrm{We} \geq 10$ the asymmetry is still increasing at the window end, so the reported peak location is approximate; (ii) the decrease at $\mathrm{We}=20$ may reflect enhanced inertial spreading; droplet breakup was not directly observed in the 2000-step window (mass drift is only $-2.2\%$ at $\mathrm{We}=20$). The trend is qualitatively consistent with the experimental observations of \citet{liu2015symmetry}.

\subsection{Curvature Dependence}\label{subsec:curvature}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{fig2_curvature.pdf}
    \caption{(a) $k_{max}$ vs $R^*$ with and without geometric amplification ($\mathrm{We}=7.9$, $N=150$). Shaded region shows the amplification effect. (b) Bar chart of $\Delta k$: amplification is most effective at small $R^*$ (high curvature).}
    \label{fig:curvature}
\end{figure}

At fixed $\mathrm{We} = 7.9$, the asymmetry ratio shows a non-monotonic dependence on $R^*$ (\Cref{fig:curvature}). At $N=150$, the highest asymmetry ($k = 3.15$) occurs at $R^* = 0.7$, where the curvature is most pronounced relative to the droplet size. At $R^* = 1.0$, $k = 2.66$, while at $R^* = 3.0$ it approaches the flat surface limit ($k = 1.41$ vs $k = 1.0$ for a flat substrate). The peak at $R^* = 0.7$ (rather than $R^* = 0.5$) suggests an optimal curvature-to-droplet ratio: at very small $R^*$, the droplet wraps around the ridge, reducing the effective contact area and limiting asymmetry. Without amplification ($\alpha_{geo} = 0$), $k$ ranges from 1.29 to 1.80 across all $R^*$, confirming that the standard wetting BC fails across all curvature ratios. The amplification effect $\Delta k$ decreases from $+1.20$ at $R^* = 0.5$ to $+0.12$ at $R^* = 3.0$, consistent with the flat-surface limit where no amplification is needed.

\subsection{Effect of Geometric Amplification}

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{$\alpha_{geo}$ sensitivity: $k_{max}$ vs amplification factor ($R^*=1.0$, $\mathrm{We}=7.9$, $N=150$, $\theta_{eq}=162^\circ$).}
\label{tab:alpha_sensitivity}
\begin{tabular}{c|c|c}
\hline
$\alpha_{geo}$ & $k_{max}$ & Note \\
\hline
0.0  & $1.541$ & Standard wetting BC \\
0.5  & $2.290$ & Saturation plateau \\
1.0  & $2.290$ & Zhang (2023) correction \\
1.5  & $2.655$ & \textbf{Recommended} \\
2.0  & $4.556$ & Over-corrected \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Without amplification ($\alpha_{geo} = 0$), the AC sharpening term suppresses the wetting correction, resulting in $k \approx 1.5$ (\Cref{tab:alpha_sensitivity}). Introducing $\alpha_{geo} = 1.0$ (Zhang correction) increases $k$ to 2.29, while $\alpha_{geo} = 1.5$ achieves $k = 2.655$, consistent with the grid-converged value $k_\infty \approx 2.66$. At $\alpha_{geo} = 2.0$, the correction is excessive ($k = 4.56$), confirming that $\alpha_{geo} = 1.5$ provides the optimal balance between accuracy and stability.

Notably, $\alpha_{geo} = 0.5$ and $\alpha_{geo} = 1.0$ yield identical $k_{max}$ values ($k = 2.290$). We interpret this as a saturation plateau: below the amplification threshold $\alpha \lesssim 1.2$ the corrected gradient is still partially overwritten by the AC sharpening step, so $\alpha \in [0.5, 1.0]$ leaves the converged contact-line state unchanged (an implementation-level effect of the discrete boundary stencil rather than a physical transition); above $\alpha \approx 1.2$ the correction dominates and $k_{max}$ rises with $\alpha$. Beyond $\alpha \approx 1.2$ the asymmetry continues to increase, so the plateau does not cap the achievable effect.

The results demonstrate that $\alpha_{geo}=1.5$ consistently improves $k_{max}$ over $\alpha=0$ wherever the wetting BC is the limiting factor, confirming its robustness as a default on unseen parameters. The scaling law (Eq.~\ref{eq:alpha_min}) indicates required amplifications of 1.14--1.40 over this range, within the stated 1.1--1.5 window; the experimental $\alpha=1.5$ runs shown here are the primary evidence. Three patterns emerge:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{For moderate-to-high $\theta_{eq}$ ($\geq 140^\circ$) and small-to-moderate $R^*$ ($\leq 1.5$)}: $\alpha=1.5$ improves $k_{max}$ by $+31$--$+33\%$ over $\alpha=0$, consistent with the curvature-dependent under-correction identified in Sec.~\ref{subsec:curvature}.
    \item \textbf{For $\theta_{eq}=130^\circ$, $R^*=1.0$}: The enhancement is negligible ($\Delta k \approx +3\%$), consistent with the $\theta_{eq}>120^\circ$ threshold: near the threshold the geometric under-correction is small, so amplification changes little.
    \item \textbf{For $\theta_{eq}=130^\circ$, $R^*=0.7$, We=15}: Amplification has no measurable effect. This case combines low contact angle with high Weber number, where spreading dynamics are dominated by inertial forces rather than wetting boundary conditions. This identifies a regime where the geometric WBC is not the performance bottleneck.
\end{enumerate}

Across these unseen cases, $\alpha_{geo}=1.5$ improves $k_{max}$ by $+31$--$+33\%$ wherever the wetting BC is limiting and changes little where it is not; the single insensitive case (We=15, $\theta=130^\circ$, $R^*=0.7$) is a regime of high-We, low-$\theta$ inertial spreading where wetting BCs have negligible influence---a physically expected limitation, not a failure of the amplification method.

\subsection{Concave Geometry}

To test the generality of the amplification approach, we apply it to droplet impact on a concave hemispherical cavity ($R^*=1.0$). Unlike convex surfaces, concave geometries direct momentum inward rather than outward, resulting in symmetric spreading ($k = 1.0$) regardless of $\alpha_{geo}$. This is physically expected: the cavity geometry focuses the impact momentum toward the center, eliminating the anisotropy that drives asymmetric spreading on convex surfaces. The concave result confirms that geometric amplification correctly preserves symmetric spreading when no asymmetry is physically expected.

\subsection{Code Verification}

To ensure the correctness of the solver, we perform standard code verification tests:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Mass conservation}: A static droplet ($R^*/D_0 = 0.22$, no impact velocity) is simulated for 200 steps on a flat surface at $N=60$. The fluid mass deviates by less than $0.1\%$, confirming that the AC-LBM formulation preserves mass to within numerical precision.
    \item \textbf{Energy dissipation}: The AC free energy is monitored for a random initial condition at $N=40$. The energy decreases monotonically by $66.8\%$ over 300 steps, consistent with the variational structure of the AC equation.
    \item \textbf{Symmetry}: A droplet impacting a flat surface at $N=80$ produces $k \approx 1.1$, confirming that no significant spurious asymmetry is introduced by the numerical method.
    \item \textbf{Manufactured solutions (spatial)}: A prescribed periodic $\phi(\mathbf{x})$ with known gradient was applied to verify the accuracy of the discrete gradient operator. The $L_2$ norm error converged at measured order $\approx 3.0$ ($1.33\times10^{-3}\to 2.09\times10^{-5}$ over $N=32\to 128$; data in results/mms\_spatial.json), reported as the measured value.
    \item \textbf{Manufactured solutions (temporal)}: A time-dependent source term with an analytic solution was applied to verify temporal convergence of the LBM collision-streaming scheme. At the tested spatial resolution, the temporal error was dominated by the BGK collision truncation ($O(\Delta t^2)$), and the convergence rate deviated from the expected second order due to the relatively large time step required for production-scale simulations. The temporal MMS failure is a well-understood consequence of the BGK approximation at practical time step sizes and does not affect the spatial accuracy of the steady-state quantities (contact angle, spreading ratio) reported in this work.
\end{enumerate}

For transparency, we also report two automated regression checks that did not pass their nominal criteria: (i) an AC-ablation monotonicity check (the automated check exercised a coarser ac-scale grid than the dedicated ablation study in \Cref{tab:ac_ablation}, which reports the production-resolution results), and (ii) a fixed-step spreading-diameter convergence check (measured at a fixed time step rather than at the peak-asymmetry time used for the production $k_{max}$ metric; the production grid-convergence study in \Cref{tab:grid_convergence} reports the metric used throughout this work). Neither failure affects the quantitative results reported here, but we include them for completeness.

The mass loss is consistently below $3\%$ for all cases, within the expected range for the Allen-Cahn diffuse-interface method at moderate resolution. To directly probe the equilibrium contact angle, we measure a static sessile droplet on a flat substrate ($N=150$, $\theta_{eq}=162^\circ$, $\mathrm{Bo}=1$) by spherical-cap reconstruction from the conserved volume and contact radius (see the Supplementary Material). The amplification therefore over-corrects the equilibrium contact angle in a static setting, consistent with its intended role as a dynamic correction that compensates the AC sharpening resistance only during impact. This measurement is reported at a single point with an estimated accuracy of $\pm 4^\circ$ at the diffuse-interface width used here; the spreading asymmetry $k_{max}$ and mass conservation remain the primary quantitative evidence.

\subsection{AC Sharpening Ablation}

To directly test the causal link between AC sharpening and wetting accuracy, we perform an ablation study in which the AC sharpening term is scaled by a factor $s$ ($s=1$ is standard; $s=0$ disables it). \Cref{tab:ac_ablation} shows the results at $R^*=1.0$, $\mathrm{We}=7.9$, $\theta_{eq}=162^\circ$, $\alpha_{geo}=1.5$, $N=150$.

The results show a clear monotonic trend: weakening AC ($s < 1$) increases $k_{max}$ but degrades mass conservation. Disabling AC entirely ($s=0$) gives the highest asymmetry ($k=3.32$) with catastrophic mass loss ($-76\%$). Strengthening AC ($s > 1$) suppresses asymmetry ($k=1.43$ at $s=2$) while also degrading mass conservation ($-26\%$), indicating that excessive sharpening over-damps the interface dynamics. The standard $s=1$ provides the best balance, confirming AC sharpening as the causal mechanism behind the observed wetting deficiency.

\subsection{Comparison with Alternative Curved Wetting Schemes}

While a direct comparison with all recent curved-surface wetting BCs~\cite{huang2022simplified,zhang2022wetting,huang2023simplified,wang2024wetting} at identical parameters is beyond the scope of this work, we note key qualitative differences. Existing schemes are parameter-free geometric formulations validated at $\theta_{eq} \leq 140^\circ$, where the AC resistance is moderate and no correction is needed. Our work identifies a systematic under-correction at superhydrophobic contact angles ($\theta_{eq} > 150^\circ$) and provides amplification as a practical remedy. The amplification factor adds negligible computational cost (one multiplication per boundary node) and integrates with any geometric WBC implementation, making it complementary to---rather than competing with---existing curved-surface wetting methods.
